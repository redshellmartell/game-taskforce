## Owner's idea (as given)

A role-playing game based on a personality test like 16Personalities. Players answer questions, then choose one of the avatars from their results. The test is the character-building step. The game is designed to get people to roleplay as different archetypes instead of different characters.

## What the owner has fixed

- Genre: role-playing game.
- Character creation = answering a personality questionnaire. There are no stat blocks or classes to pick from a list; the answers produce the result.
- The result offers a choice of avatars (players pick one from their results, not one forced type).
- The point of play is roleplaying an archetype (a way of thinking and acting), not a named character with a backstory.
- Inspiration: the 16-type personality model (four two-sided traits giving 16 combinations).

## IP constraint (Manager note)

16Personalities is a third-party, trademarked site, and MBTI / Myers-Briggs are trademarks too. The game must use its **own original questionnaire, trait names and archetype names**, inspired by the general idea of a 16-type model. Do not copy their questions, type names (e.g. "Architect", "Mediator"), descriptions, art or the four-letter codes as branding.

## Open questions for research to answer or bring back to the owner

The owner has not specified these; research should propose options, not decide them:
- Format: tabletop with cards/booklet (the studio's usual output) or something else.
- Player count and play time.
- Whether there is a game master or it is GM-less.
- How "from the results" works: how many avatars a player is offered (e.g. their top match plus neighbours).
- What the archetype actually changes in play (abilities, scene goals, how the player is rewarded for acting in type).
- Audience: party / icebreaker, story gamers, or groups like classrooms and teams.

## Research focus

Comparable games that build characters through questions or personality (e.g. lifepath generators, Ultima IV's virtue questions, GM-less story games, party roleplay games), how players and reviewers feel about personality-test framing, and whether "play an archetype, not a character" is an open gap.

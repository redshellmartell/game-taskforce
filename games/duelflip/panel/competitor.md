# Review: Duel Flip
## The Competitor

### First impression
Clean teaching, solid hook. The shared river with bait mechanics is elegant, and forcing a second flip kills the "safe two-flip exit" grind. But in one read I spotted the problem: you always leave your lowest card, which the design notes admit they're watching for. It's not a decision—it's following the math.

### Best moment and worst moment
**Best:** In the casual vs. competitor game (T9), I clashed my opponent's bait and took their pile plus the bait back. Direct, punishing, and entirely my choice to push into it. That's the game's teeth.

**Worst:** Same game, T0-T2. Three clashes in my first two turns (Ur3, Ke4, both busts). I did nothing wrong—just unlucky. It felt like my only decision was whether to spend my Lifebuoy, and twice I was already bust. Clash variance can overshadow skill early.

### Confusing rules
None. The rules are tight and precise. Lifebuoy timing, bait, leftover forcing—all clear.

### Pet peeves hit
**Dominant strategy (#2):** Leaving your lowest card is nearly always optimal. The worst-table log shows it relentlessly: Ke2, Ke1, An8, Sn7, St1, Ke5. There's no reason to ever leave a high card as bait because leftovers must be taken by the next banker. Yes, you could bluff to deny the opponent a species, but the math says "lowest" wins most positions. Once that strategy is found, there's one play and the illusion of decisions evaporates. The design notes flag this as a balance knob to re-test, but in this version it's solved.

### Who I enjoyed playing with
**Best table (casual vs. competitor):** Chaotic, volatile, lots of swings. Casual player busts twice into my bait, I bust twice from clashes. High interaction, leading changes on almost every turn. Fun because the tension stays live, though the outcome felt more like "who hit better clash luck" than "who played better."

**Worst table (competitor vs. family):** I pushed every turn for 4 cards to refund the Lifebuoy (T0: 3 cards, leaves; T2: 4 cards, refund; repeat). Family played cautiously. I dominated 70% win rate because the greedy pusher line beats cautious bankers hard. It was less "playing against someone" and more "executing the known strategy" while my opponent did not.

### Ratings
**Fun: 4** — Interaction is real, the bait mechanic rewards reading your opponent's tolerance, and the second-flip mandate keeps the tension high. Clashes add swing. But once you know "push to 4 and leave lowest," the play becomes routine, especially on the best table against weaker opponents. It's a good quick 2p game, not a deep one.

**Replay: 3** — The first 10 plays, you're discovering the push/bank calculus and the bait trap. After that, you're refining the same line. Species bonuses add a little variability, but "leave lowest" being solved caps replayability hard. Sea Salt & Paper stays fresh across 160+ plays; this flattens faster.

**Would buy: Maybe at $25** — I'd play it twice a month at a casual game night, and I respect the design. But for a strategist paying $25+, I want multiple routes that check each other (7 Wonders, Splendor level), not one pattern that dominates. The short play time and direct interaction earn a "maybe," not a "no." At $35+, it's a pass.

### The one change I'd make
Let players choose which of their own pile cards to leave as bait instead of always forcing the lowest. That one rule break turns a routine choice into a bluff: do you leave the high card to tempt them or the low card to avoid feeding them value? Right now it's forced. Alternatively, score species bonuses only if you hold *strictly* the same count as opponent (tie gives no bonus), which forces real tension on which species to leave. The current +8 at majority is generous, but the "leave lowest" dominance means it rarely matters.

### Who I'd recommend this to
Two-player duel fanatics, especially fans of push-your-luck and direct interaction (Azul at 2p, Sea Salt & Paper fans). It works as a casino-night opener or a competition between skilled 2p players. **Not** for strategists wanting a solved game with multiple routes or hidden depth. Not a full-evening game; it's a palate cleanser.
